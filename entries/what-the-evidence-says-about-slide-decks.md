## Decide the medium and the argument before any slide {#summary}

Three conclusions come out of the research this brief collects.

**On average, having slides made no difference to learning.** A [[meta-analysis]] of 48 studies found no average difference between the same material taught with and without slides ([Baker et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2018.08.003)). In a simulated board pitch, audiences rated a slide deck no better than a talk with no visuals ([Moulton, Türkay and Kosslyn, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0178774)). Where studies do find a difference, it comes from what is on the slides.

**The evidence points to different amounts of text for a deck presented live and a deck read alone.** When a speaker sets the pace, long text on the slide competes with the voice. When a reader sets the pace, there is no voice, and the page has to carry the explanation. This evidence is indirect: it comes from studies of students learning, and no study compares the two kinds of deck directly.

**Most advice on structure is practice, and many popular numbers are folklore.** This review found no test of the rule that a deck should state its answer first, against a slow build, on people who make decisions. The 10-minute attention span and the claim that stories are 22 times more memorable have no source. Several well-known findings from behavioural science failed when repeated.

The brief ends with [a nine-step sequence for planning a deck](#sequence), which is its own synthesis of the evidence and the canon.

> [!NOTE] A companion skill for Claude turns this research into a working method for planning and reviewing decks: [SlidePartner, in the claude-skills repository](https://github.com/juliedavila/claude-skills/tree/main/skills/SlidePartner). Its evidence file carries the full source list.

Two terms recur. A **debrief** is a deck whose purpose is understanding: the audience should come away knowing what was found or how something works. A **decision deck** asks for a decision. Most of the experimental evidence concerns the first, and most of the practitioner canon concerns the second.

## On average slides change nothing, and what is on them does

The meta-analysis above pooled 48 studies that taught the same content with slides and without. The average difference was close to zero: an [[effect size]] of g = 0.067, with a margin of error wide enough to include both a small benefit and a small harm. School pupils gained from slides and college students did not. The authors conclude that research should ask how slides are used, because their presence alone explains little.

The closest study to a business setting had participants build and deliver live pitches to others who played a board. Each pitch was randomly assigned a slide deck, a zooming presentation tool, or no visual aid. Audiences rated the slide deck the same as the unaided talk, and memory for the case did not differ by format ([Moulton, Türkay and Kosslyn, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0178774)). A well-argued talk with no slides is a legitimate choice.

What sits on the slide does matter. In one controlled experiment, 110 engineering students watched the same recorded talk with one of two slide sets. Students who saw a full-sentence headline over visual evidence scored higher on comprehension than students who saw a topic title over bullets. The difference was large by the usual convention (d = 0.81, where 0.8 counts as large). They also wrote fewer misconceptions and recalled more ten days later ([Garner and Alley, 2013](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol29-6/23_ijee2791ns.pdf)). The study was run by the authors of the method it tests. It changed the headline and the slide body together, so it cannot say which did the work. A later study in a medical school compared two year groups, without random assignment, and found gains in the same direction ([Issa et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.12127)).

Every deck in one sample of 140 broke basic rules of perception and memory. The sample covered research, education, government and business. The common flaws were text too small to read, too much on a slide, and changes of colour or layout that meant nothing. Business decks were no better than academic ones ([Kosslyn et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00230)). That paper describes practice and did not test whether the flaws harm understanding.

## A presented deck and a deck read alone call for different amounts of text

The experimental result most useful to someone who writes both pre-reads and presentations concerns pacing. A talk is [[system-paced]]: the audience cannot stop or go back. A pre-read is [[self-paced]].

**Design had larger effects when the audience could not control the pace.** An overview of 29 reviews covering 1,189 studies found that design principles had larger effects for complex material and in system-paced settings such as lectures than in self-paced ones ([Noetel et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211052329)).

**Spoken explanation beat printed explanation only when the presenter set the pace.** Pictures with narration teach better than pictures with printed text in short, system-paced lessons. A meta-analysis found that the advantage depends on pacing ([Ginns, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2005.07.001)). In one self-paced classroom study the effect reversed, and students learned more from printed text than from narration ([Tabbers, Martens and van Merriënboer, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1348/000709904322848824)).

**A reader who set the pace understood as well as a listener, and inferred more.** Across 46 studies, reading and listening comprehension did not differ overall. Self-paced reading came out slightly ahead, and reading had a clearer advantage on questions that required inference ([Clinton-Lisell, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211060871)).

**While a speaker is talking, dense slides take attention from the speech.** Students recalled less of what a lecturer said when text-heavy slides were on screen ([Wecker, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2012.01.013)). When a narrator is speaking, printing the same words in full beside a figure hurts learning. A few on-screen terms that echo the narration help ([Mayer and Johnson, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380); [Adesope and Nesbit, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026147)).

> [!SYNTHESIS] The table below is this brief's inference from those findings. The first three rows follow from the studies above. The last two are practitioner advice with no finding behind them.

| | Presented live | Read alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who sets the pace | The speaker | The reader |
| Words on the slide | A headline that states the claim, and a few terms the speaker also says | Full sentences beside the part of the visual they explain |
| Where the explanation lives | In the speaker's voice | On the page |
| How to read a chart | The speaker says it | Printed on the chart's page |
| What must be remembered | Kept on screen | On a page the reader can return to |

Practitioners reached a similar conclusion from experience. Nancy Duarte separates a presentation from a [slide document meant to be read](https://www.duarte.com/slidedocs/). Andrew Abela separates a style for a large room with a speaker from a style for a small group that discusses printed pages. Whether one file can serve both jobs is disputed among them, and this review found no study that compares a hybrid deck with two purpose-built ones.

> [!SYNTHESIS] A pre-read followed by a meeting is best treated as two decks. The pre-read carries the argument in full sentences. The meeting deck is short: a one-page recap, the questions that are still open, and the decision. This follows from the pacing evidence and from practitioner accounts of meetings that start from questions. This review found no experiment that tests it.

> [!GAP] This review found no published figure for how many people read an internal pre-read, how long they spend on it, or whether they open speaker notes. It found no eye-tracking study of business readers going through a deck alone.

## The structure canon agrees with itself, and this review found few tests of it

Practitioner methods for structuring a deck come from consulting, the military, intelligence analysis, science and speechwriting. The books themselves were not read for this brief, so this section rests on public summaries of them.

**State the answer first.** Barbara Minto's pyramid principle puts one governing thought at the top and groups the supporting reasons beneath it. Military writing calls the same rule "bottom line up front". In the US Army's briefing doctrine, a decision briefing opens with the problem and the recommendation, and the facts and analysis follow ([Field Manual 6-0, 2014](https://archive.org/details/fm-6-0-commander-and-staff-organization-and-operations-2014)). The US intelligence community's analytic standards require the main judgments up front, facts kept apart from judgments, and likelihood kept apart from confidence ([Intelligence Community Directive 203](https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf)).

**Write each title as a claim.** Consulting practice calls this an action title: a headline that states the slide's conclusion as a sentence. The test is to read only the titles, in order, and see whether they make the argument. The canon on scientific presentations reached the same rule separately. It is the structural rule closest to having an experiment behind it, in the Garner and Alley study above, which changed the slide body along with the title.

**Plan the claims before building slides.** The consulting, speechwriting and design traditions each prescribe it under a different name: a storyboard, a ghost deck, sticky notes, planning away from the software.

**Story methods and answer-first methods overlap.** Both want a tension the audience recognizes at the start. Duarte, whose best-known method alternates between what is and what could be, advises a different order for senior audiences: [give the summary first and let the group drive](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/).

> [!GAP] This review found no experiment that presents the same business content answer-first and as a build-up to people who make decisions, and measures understanding, agreement or the quality of the decision. The central rule of executive communication rests on practice. The nearest evidence is indirect: stating a conclusion persuades more than implying it, and a frame given before a passage aids comprehension.

Critics of slides and their defenders condemn the same habit. Edward Tufte argued that the bulleted outline breaks up reasoning. His example was a [briefing slide from the Columbia shuttle investigation](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/columbia-accident-investigation-board-the-boeing-powerpoint-slide/), in which the deciding caveat sat in small type under a reassuring title. The investigation board cited that slide as one illustration of poor technical communication. It listed organizational causes for the accident and did not find that a slide caused it. Replies to Tufte hold that the fault lies in topic titles over nested fragments, and that slides can carry sentences ([Farkas, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1075/idj.14.2.08far)).

## The evidence supports a journey whose destination is announced

A common intuition holds that a good deck takes its audience on a journey. Two findings from research on reading and memory bear on it. Neither was measured on decks.

**A frame given first aided comprehension.** In a classic set of experiments, telling people what a passage was about before they read it improved comprehension and recall. Telling them afterwards did much less ([Bransford and Johnson, 1972](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(72)80006-9)).

**People remembered answers they were curious about.** In a study using trivia questions, people recalled answers better when they had been curious about the question ([Kang et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x)). One account of curiosity holds that it comes from a gap a person can see between what they know and what they want to know ([Loewenstein, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75)).

**Suspense has little support.** Duarte's method of alternating between the present and a proposed future came from reading celebrated speeches. No set of unsuccessful speeches was read for comparison. Studies of whether knowing an ending spoils a story disagree with each other, and all of them concern fiction.

> [!SYNTHESIS] The version of a journey that this evidence permits: say where the deck is going and what it asks of the audience, then let each section open with the question it answers, and make each step follow from the one before. The audience knows the destination and still has a gap to close at every step. Hiding the destination is advised, by Duarte and by speech coaches generally, for a live talk to a large audience that has yet to make up its mind.

## Four findings from learning research apply to explaining complex material

All four were measured on students, mostly in short lessons. The second column says what each suggests for a slide, which is this brief's reading.

| Finding | What it suggests for a slide | Size of the effect | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cues that mark what matters help | Highlight the one element the title is about | d about 0.38 across 29 studies | [Alpizar, Adesope and Wong, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7) |
| Words placed next to the thing they describe help | Label the chart directly and drop the legend | g = 0.63 across 58 comparisons | [Schroeder and Cenkci, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9) |
| An explanation presented in segments helps | Build a dense diagram across several slides | Small to medium across 56 investigations | [Rey et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4) |
| Learning the names of the parts before the mechanism helps | Name the components before explaining how they interact | Median d = 0.85 in the lab that first reported it | [Mayer, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-3185-5_31) |

The first three figures are from independent meta-analyses. For the same principles, the lab that first reported them gives figures about twice as large, from its own short lessons. The fourth figure is that lab's own and has no independent estimate in this review, so it probably overstates the effect.

**Help for newcomers can slow experts.** Definitions and step-by-step build-up that help a novice become unnecessary or harmful for someone who knows the domain ([Kalyuga, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3)). This review found no study of how much to remove for an expert audience.

## For uncertain findings, numbers beat probability words

**A number beside a probability word improved agreement.** In an experiment with 1,202 participants, numeric formats and combined word-and-number formats produced better agreement between sender and receiver than probability words alone ([Mandel and Irwin, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1930297500008603)).

**A numeric range cost little trust.** Across five experiments, giving a number with a range lowered trust in the number only slightly, and vague verbal hedging lowered it more ([van der Bles et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1913678117)).

One finding from outside decks is suggestive for accounts of what happened. Clinical handoff formats put the situation first and owned actions last. A structured handoff programme across nine hospitals cut medical errors by 23% ([Starmer et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1405556)). That result belongs to a training programme in hospitals, and this review found no test of the same order in a business deck.

## A few persuasion findings bear on a deck that asks for a decision

Persuasion research is large and mostly conducted on students and consumers. Read it with one caution first. A review of 30 meta-analyses of message design found small average differences, and the range of plausible effects for a new case usually included zero ([O'Keefe and Hoeken, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.664160)). No structural choice guarantees agreement.

The third column of the table is this brief's synthesis.

| Finding | Kind of evidence | What it suggests for a decision deck | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A stated conclusion and a specific recommendation persuade more than an implied one | Meta-analyses, small effect | Write the ask as an action with an owner, a date and a cost | Summarized in [O'Keefe and Hoeken, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.664160) |
| Answering the opposing argument beats ignoring it, and raising it unanswered does worst | Two meta-analyses, small effect | Give the strongest objection its own slide, with the answer | [O'Keefe, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1080/23808985.1999.11678963); [Allen, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1080/10570319109374395) |
| Weak arguments lower the weight of strong ones | Repeated experiments | Keep only the reasons the author would stake the case on | [Weaver, Garcia and Schwarz, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1086/664497); [Obermaier and Koch, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-73348-1) |
| Whatever is presented as the default gets chosen far more often | Meta-analysis of 58 studies, d = 0.68 | Doing nothing is the default in most meetings, so show it as one option with its cost and date | [Jachimowicz et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2018.43) |
| A product was chosen less often when shown alone than beside an alternative | One paper | Show two or three real alternatives beside the recommendation | [Mochon, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1086/671343) |
| Volunteering a weakness raises credibility | Meta-analysis of two-sided advertising | State the main weakness, with what will be done about it | [Eisend, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2005.11.001) |
| People underestimate their own timelines | Repeated experiments | Back the timeline with how long comparable work took | [Buehler, Griffin and Ross, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366) |

Field research on managers who sell an issue upward found that the successful ones tied it to goals the leaders already held, involved others early, chose their timing and came with a solution ([Dutton et al., 2001](https://doi.org/10.5465/3069412)). Building agreement in conversations before the meeting is near-universal practitioner advice, and this review found no controlled test of it. Groups also tend to discuss what everyone already knows and neglect what only one member knows ([Stasser and Titus, 1985](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.48.6.1467)), which is a reason for a deck to state plainly what is new.

## A story's effect on belief outlasts a statistic's, and stories lower scrutiny

**Stories and statistics roughly tie on persuasion.** Meta-analyses reach different summaries depending on what is measured and how involved the audience is ([Xu, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/10410236.2022.2137750); [Freling et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.01.006)). Most of that work concerns health messages.

**A story's effect on belief lasted longer.** In controlled experiments, the effect of a statistic on belief faded by 73% over a day, and the effect of a story faded by 32% ([Graeber, Roth and Zimmermann, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae020)). When a decision is made days after a presentation, a number paired with a concrete case has a better chance of surviving.

**Absorption in a story lowers counter-arguing.** People who are absorbed in a narrative find fewer faults in it ([Green and Brock, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701); [van Laer et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1086/673383)). That is one account of how narrative persuades, and it is a reason to give a decision audience more than the story.

> [!SYNTHESIS] A working test for honest use of narrative in a decision deck has six parts. Every claim the story rests on appears where the audience can check it. A single case comes with a statement of how common such cases are. The real downside is stated plainly. Every option shown is one the author would accept. Uncertainty is given as a range. The author would be comfortable describing the deck's structure out loud. The test follows a common account of the line between persuasion and manipulation, in which manipulation works by getting around a person's capacity to deliberate ([Sunstein, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1561/107.00000014)).

## Many rules quoted in presentation advice have no source or did not replicate

A [[replication]] repeats a study to see whether the result holds. The measure r in this table is a correlation: 0 means no relationship and 1 a perfect one.

| Popular claim | What the record shows | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Visuals are processed 60,000 times faster than text" | Writers who traced the claim reached promotional material with no named study ([PolicyViz](https://policyviz.com/2015/09/17/the-60000-fallacy/)) | No source found |
| "Stories are 22 times more memorable than facts" | This review found no source in the work of either person the figure is credited to | No source found |
| Fixed limits on slides, minutes, type size or words per line | This review found no study testing any of them | No source found |
| "Audiences stop paying attention after 10 minutes" | Two reviews found no such threshold in the primary data ([Wilson and Korn, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/00986280701291291); [Bradbury, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00109.2016)) | Not supported |
| "Seven plus or minus two bullets" | A real estimate of short-term memory, misapplied: the current estimate is about four items, and neither figure came from a study of slides ([Cowan, 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922)) | Misapplied |
| "One identifiable person moves people more than a statistic" | The combined effect across studies is small (r = 0.05; [Lee and Feeley, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/15534510.2016.1216891)), and a replication planned in advance found none ([Maier, Wong and Feldman, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.90203)) | Small or absent |
| "Charts make a claim more believable" | Four replications with 623 participants found no added persuasion ([Dragicevic and Jansen, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2017.2744298)) | Did not replicate |
| "Experts persuade more when they sound unsure" | A large replication, with its method fixed in advance, found that plain confidence helped ([Løhre et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/gjxyh)) | Did not replicate |
| "A decoy option steers the choice" | The effect was absent with realistic options ([Frederick, Lee and Baskin, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.12.0061)) | Did not replicate reliably |
| "Frame it as a loss" | Across 94 studies, loss-framed messages showed no general persuasive advantage ([Gallagher and Updegraff, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-011-9308-7)) | No average effect |
| "Too many options paralyse" | The average effect across 50 experiments is about zero ([Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235)) | No average effect |

Of the eleven, three have no source this review could find, two misread or outrun their sources, and six rest on findings that shrank or vanished when repeated or pooled.

## Almost none of this was tested on people who decide

> [!GAP] Nearly every experiment in this brief tested students learning unfamiliar material for a test minutes later, or members of the public reading a short message. This review found five studies of professionals judging real proposals: one of managers, three of investors and one of film producers.

**Managers.** 76 managers each saw one version of a business strategy with identical text, as bullets or as a visualization. Those who saw a visualization rated the presentation and the presenter more favourably, and understood the strategy no better ([Kernbach, Eppler and Bresciani, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/2329488414525444)).

**Investors.** Investors funded presenters who looked prepared ([Chen, Yao and Kotha, 2009](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2009.36462018)). Gestures that depicted the idea raised investor interest in a pitch ([Clarke, Cornelissen and Healey, 2019](https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2016.1008)). Early-stage investors decided largely on their read of the person ([Huang and Pearce, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0001839215597270)).

**Film producers.** People hearing a pitch in Hollywood judged the person pitching as much as the idea ([Elsbach and Kramer, 2003](https://doi.org/10.2307/30040623)).

Fieldwork on strategy teams shows a use of decks that comprehension tests do not measure: the deck as a working object that a team edits, reorders and negotiates around ([Kaplan, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.1100.0531)).

What this review did not find:

- A comparison of answer-first and build-up order for people who make decisions.
- A comparison of any two orders of a whole deck.
- A comparison of the same content as a deck read alone and as a deck presented, or of a slide document and a prose memo.
- A test of claim titles with the slide body held constant.
- A test of one idea per slide, of word limits, or of agenda and summary slides.
- A test of whether stories help or hurt with an audience that must justify its decision.

## Nine steps put the evidence and the canon in working order {#sequence}

> [!SYNTHESIS] This sequence is this brief's reading of the evidence and the canon together. The medium comes first because it decides how every later step is written down. Treat each step as a default to adapt.

1. **Decide the medium.** A deck is presented, read alone, or both. If a pre-read is followed by a meeting, plan two decks.
2. **Decide the purpose.** Understanding or a decision. For a decision, write the ask as an action with an owner, a date and a cost.
3. **Write the point in one sentence** with a position and what is at stake. Then write the three sentences a decision maker would use to justify the decision to someone else.
4. **Open with the frame.** The situation the audience accepts, what disturbs it, the question that raises, and the answer.
5. **Write every title as a claim, and read the titles alone.** They should make the argument as a paragraph. Fix the list before building any slide.
6. **Give each claim its evidence and cut the weak reasons.** Move supporting detail behind the main line.
7. **For a decision, add what a sceptic needs.** The strongest objection answered, doing nothing as an option with its cost, the main weakness stated, and a timeline grounded in comparable work.
8. **Render for the medium.** Few words and a speaker for the room. Full sentences beside each visual for the reader.
9. **Test it on someone who was not in the work.**

## Glossary

<dl class="glossary">
<dt>Meta-analysis</dt><dd>A study that combines the results of many separate studies of the same question into one average estimate.</dd>
<dt>Effect size</dt><dd>A standardized measure of the difference between two groups. For the measures d and g used here, 0.2 is conventionally small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large.</dd>
<dt>Replication</dt><dd>A repeat of an earlier study to see whether its result holds. A preregistered or registered replication fixes its method and analysis in public before collecting data.</dd>
<dt>System-paced</dt><dd>Delivered at a pace the audience cannot control, such as a live talk or a video without pause.</dd>
<dt>Self-paced</dt><dd>Read or viewed at a pace the reader controls, with the ability to stop and go back.</dd>
</dl>

## How this was made

**Question.** How should information be presented in a slide deck when the purpose is to debrief complex material or to win a decision, and how should a deck that is read alone differ from one that is presented? Set by Julie Davila in November 2026.

**Process.** The research was done in a conversation with Claude on 25 November 2026. A planning agent split the question into four lines of inquiry: the practitioner canon on structure, the experimental evidence on slides and comprehension, the difference between a presented deck and one read alone, and persuasion and decision-making. Four research agents worked the lines in parallel using web search, and read abstracts and open full texts through Crossref, Semantic Scholar and the Internet Archive. They recorded 143 findings, each with a source, a note on whether the source itself was read, and a strength grade. The planning agent reconciled the four sets of notes and drafted this brief. No structured literature screen was run, so the searches were broad and were not systematic.

**What was checked and what was not.** Every digital object identifier (DOI) linked here was resolved against the Crossref registry and matched to its author, year and title. The figures from Baker et al., Clinton-Lisell, van der Bles et al. and Graeber et al. were confirmed against the papers' abstracts. Most other figures come from abstracts or full texts the research agents read. A figure seen only in a search result was left out, which is why some rows describe a result in words. The canonical books (Minto, Duarte, Abela) were not read: what this brief says about them rests on public summaries, and it quotes none of them. Web pages without a DOI were found through search and were not fetched again for this brief. Passages marked *Synthesis*, and the table columns labelled as this brief's reading, are its own reasoning. Statements that this review found no test mean that these searches did not find one.

**Adversarial review and cold read (25 November 2026, before publication).** A model from a different vendor (OpenAI GPT, run read-only through Codex) checked the draft against the research notes and, where it could reach them, the sources. It raised 13 findings and did not complete a claim-by-claim tally. Twelve led to changes: the draft's claims that slides add nothing and that the two media need opposite rules were narrowed to what the studies show; one cohort study had been described as several; and attributions about story methods, silent reading, a single-option study and the mechanism of narrative persuasion were narrowed or removed. One finding, about the order of the Army decision briefing, was checked against the manual's text and the brief's description was kept and made more exact. A separate agent then read the draft as a newcomer and found 14 problems of clarity and consistency, including a contradiction between the title and the planning sequence and a sentence about on-screen text that had lost its condition. All were fixed.
