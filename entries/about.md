## A person owns each question, and AI agents do the research

**A person sets the question and owns the result.** Julie Davila chooses each question, sets its scope, reads the drafts, sends work back, and decides whether a brief is published. She is accountable for what appears here.

**AI agents do most of the research and drafting.** The agents are built on Anthropic's Claude models. One agent breaks the question into separate lines of inquiry and gives each to a research agent. Those agents search the public web, open primary sources, and write their notes to disk with a source link and an evidence grade for each finding. The planning agent then reconciles the notes, resolves conflicts between sources, and drafts the brief.

**A literature tool screens the academic work.** For the peer-reviewed side, the agents use [Elicit](https://elicit.com) to search and screen the research literature and pull findings from each included paper. Where a brief relies on an Elicit screen, it says how many papers were included and whether full texts were read.

**Some briefs get an adversarial check.** For some briefs, a separate review pass, sometimes run on a model from a different vendor, checks the load-bearing claims against their sources and hunts for errors. A brief says whether it had one.

## What every brief carries

- **The answer first.** Each brief opens with what it concludes, then the evidence.
- **A source for every external figure.** Numbers and quotations link to where they came from. A figure the agents could see only in a search result, without opening the source itself, is marked as such or left out.
- **Labelled synthesis.** Where a brief goes beyond its sources and draws its own conclusions, the passage is marked **Synthesis**. Treat those passages as reasoned hypotheses, not findings.
- **A "How this was made" section** at its end: the question, the tools, the number of sources, what was checked and what was not.

## How the evidence is graded

| Grade | What it means | How much weight it bears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peer-reviewed | Published in a refereed venue | The most, within the study's own sample and method |
| Primary industry | An organization reporting its own results, on its own blog or in its own report | Real experience, self-reported, rarely audited |
| Vendor | A company that sells a product in the area, reporting data that supports buying it | Directional only; the incentive is named beside the figure |
| Secondary | A third party reporting someone else's result | Only as good as the original, which was not read |
| Practitioner | Widely argued or practised, with little public measurement | A starting point to test |
| Synthesis | An inference drawn in the brief itself | A hypothesis to check against your own data |

## Where the method falls short

AI agents make mistakes a careful human researcher would also make, and a few a human would not. They can misread a table, lift a figure from a summary rather than the paper, or join two sources too eagerly. The steps above narrow those risks but do not remove them. When a finding matters to a decision, open the source it links to.

The briefs use public sources only. None of them contains any organization's private data, and none describes a specific organization's internal program.
