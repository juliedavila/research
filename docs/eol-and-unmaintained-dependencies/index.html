End-of-life and unmaintained dependencies are one debt with three costs

An abandoned library is a security risk, a growing technical debt and a recurring drain on developer attention at the same time. The published accounts favour one response for all three, which is to make upgrading continuous, owned and cheap, rather than a periodic emergency.

After reading this brief you can explain why end-of-life and unmaintained libraries are a different risk from known vulnerabilities, how deferred upgrades compound as debt, what the research says about the tools and practices teams use, and which responses fit which situation.

The evidence points to one response for all three costs

The risk is the next vulnerability nobody upstream will fix. A library or runtime that has reached its end of life, or whose maintainers have quietly stopped, may carry no known vulnerability today. What it has lost is someone to fix the next one, which leaves the downstream team to override, fork, replace or pay for support. Standards bodies treat this as its own category: the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) lists use of end-of-life software first in its catalogue of bad practices, and the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) now requires a management-approved plan for every technology a vendor has announced as end of life.

It is widespread. In one registry-wide study, 58.7% of npm packages showed no activity for two years. Inactivity is not the same as abandonment, but abandonment is also common among widely used packages, and downstream projects mostly do not respond when it happens.

As debt, it compounds. Each skipped release makes the eventual upgrade larger, and the upgrade usually arrives at the worst moment, forced by a security disclosure. Developers delay updates mainly for fear of breakage. Several studies find that most updates avoid detected incompatibilities, though none measures the whole cost of a migration.

For developers, the cost is attention. Update bots raise upgrade rates, but badly configured they flood teams with pull requests, many of them for dependencies the code never uses. Pruning unused dependencies, grouping, scheduling and automatic merging behind good tests are the remedies with support.

What the published accounts describe is continuous upgrading with the cost moved to the producer. Google, Spotify, GitHub and Shopify each moved from year-long upgrade projects to small, frequent bumps on shared tooling. At Google and Spotify, the team that ships a change also migrates its consumers, and compulsory deprecations carry a deadline and staffing. No controlled study tests this approach; the evidence is the organizations' own reports.

NoteThis brief complements Paving vulnerability classes, which covers engineering whole classes of vulnerability out of existence. Dependency currency is the condition that keeps a paved road paved: a safe library nobody upgrades becomes the next unmaintained dependency.

Abandonment is terminal lag, and version counts miss it

Three different situations get called "old dependencies", and they need different responses.

SituationWhat it meansWhat the downstream team can do
OutdatedNewer releases exist and the project is maintainedUpgrade; the gap closes
End of lifeThe maintainer or vendor has announced that support has endedMigrate, replace, buy extended support, or isolate
UnmaintainedNobody has announced anything; activity has simply stoppedFirst find out, then choose as for end of life

The research literature measures the first situation as technical lag: how far a project's dependencies trail their latest releases, counted in versions or in time. A common practitioner form is the libyear, the summed age of each dependency against its newest release (CHAOSS). Technical lag is resolvable when the upstream is alive. When the upstream is abandoned, it becomes terminal lag: no update will arrive, however long the team waits.

Version-based measures cannot tell the two apart. A 2026 study of 11,047 Debian packages built a maintenance-aware score from commit activity, maintainer responsiveness and repository metadata. It reclassified 62.2% of the packages that version lag alone rated low risk as high risk; those packages averaged 2,019 days since their last commit (Panter and Eisty, 2026).

How common is abandonment? Every number depends on its definition, so read them as a range:

MeasureValueSource and grade
npm packages not modified in over two years (inactivity, not confirmed abandonment)58.7% (44.3% of maintainers inactive)Zahan et al., 2022, peer-reviewed
Widely used npm packages that became abandoned over six yearsAbout 15%Miller et al., 2025, peer-reviewed
Maven dependencies whose library had halted releases14%Pashchenko et al., 2018, peer-reviewed

Abandonment also reaches packages people care about. A 2025 study of widely used npm packages found abandonment common and downstream response rare. An explicit end-of-life statement was associated with faster removal: downstream projects were 1.58 times as likely to react (Miller et al., 2025). Deprecation does not reliably end use: of 4,011 widely used deprecated npm packages, 864 kept gaining users, by an average of 14.5% a month after deprecation (Tang et al., 2026).

SynthesisAn explicit end-of-life statement is an inexpensive transparency measure with an observed association, though no study has tested it experimentally. It applies to an organization's own internal libraries as much as to open source: a dated end-of-life notice tells every consumer to move, and silence lets them stay.

You now know the three situations and why version counts hide the worst one. Next: why the security risk is different from a vulnerability count.

The security risk is the next unfixed vulnerability, not today's count

A scanner keyed only to known vulnerabilities reports an end-of-life library as clean until the day a flaw is published, and then reports a finding with no upstream fix. That is why standards treat unsupported software as a category of its own:

  • CISA's catalogue of bad practices lists use of unsupported or end-of-life software first, for any system supporting critical infrastructure (CISA).
  • NIST SP 800-53 control SA-22, Unsupported System Components, requires replacing components when support ends, or providing alternative sources of support, and discusses isolation as a way to reduce the risk of those kept (control text).
  • PCI DSS v4.0 requirement 12.3.4 requires reviewing hardware and software technologies at least every 12 months, with a senior-management-approved plan to remediate outdated technologies, including those a vendor has announced as end of life. It became mandatory on 31 March 2025 (PCI DSS v4.0.1, printed page 300).
  • The EU Cyber Resilience Act requires manufacturers of covered products to set a support period that reflects the product's expected use, at least five years unless the product is expected to be used for less, and to provide security updates through it. Most obligations apply from 11 December 2027. Open-source stewards, a defined role distinct from volunteer maintainers, must keep a documented cybersecurity policy for vulnerability handling, cooperate with market surveillance authorities, and meet limited reporting duties (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, Articles 13, 24 and 71; OpenSSF summary).
  • OWASP Top 10 folded 2021's "Vulnerable and Outdated Components" into a broader 2025 category, A03 Software Supply Chain Failures (OWASP).

The research explains why waiting does not work. Fixes rarely travel backwards: among vulnerabilities affecting several major release lines, 83.9% were fixed only in the newest line, leaving 5,056 dependents on older lines exposed (Decan et al., 2021). An older major line may lack security backports even while the project is active, so check that line's support policy and patch history before relying on it. Vulnerabilities persist downstream: critical ones last over a year on average across languages (Kumar et al., 2024), and in Maven, 58.7% of studied vulnerabilities still affected most downstream libraries when the data was collected (Zhang et al., 2023). And when a library has halted, there may be no upstream release that includes a needed fix, even when the vulnerable code underneath it has one; the way out is then to override a transitive dependency, fork, replace or buy support (Pashchenko et al., 2018).

Abandonment also opens a door that vulnerability counts never show: someone else can take the package over.

  • event-stream, 2018. The sole maintainer of a popular npm package, no longer interested in it, handed publishing rights to a volunteer, who added a dependency carrying code aimed at a cryptocurrency wallet (npm).
  • xz-utils, 2024. Over about two years, an account called "Jia Tan" won the trust of a project run largely by one person, helped by other accounts pressing for a new maintainer, then shipped a backdoor in releases 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 (CVE-2024-3094, rated 10.0) (Russ Cox's timeline).
  • Expired domains. One study found 2,818 npm maintainer email addresses on expired domains, potentially exposing 8,494 packages to takeover through account recovery (Zahan et al., 2022).

A counterweight keeps this in proportion. Dependency-level scanning overstates what an application runs: about 20% of dependencies flagged as vulnerable in one study were never deployed (Pashchenko et al., 2018), and in a function-level study 22 of 30 outdated projects never called the vulnerable code (Zapata et al., 2018). Reachability lowers the urgency of a single finding. It cannot establish safety against flaws not yet found, which may sit in code the application does call; deployment, exposed functionality and isolation still matter when ranking which end-of-life dependency to deal with first.

Evidence gapComponent matching in software composition analysis works from vulnerability databases, so an end-of-life library with no published vulnerability can pass unflagged. Some scanners add lifecycle checks; Trivy, for example, can fail a scan on an end-of-life operating system (Trivy docs). Coverage of language libraries varies by tool, and lifecycle data can also come from the open endoflife.date dataset or vendor support calendars. This review's searches found no study measuring how often scanners miss end-of-life components.

Deferred upgrades compound like interest

The 2018 tertiary review of technical-debt types cited here does not name end-of-life dependency debt as a distinct category; it falls across build debt, infrastructure debt and security debt (Rios et al., 2018). A debt register organized by that taxonomy may have no natural row for an end-of-life framework.

The interest is real, and Google's engineering book describes how it accrues. A team imports a library once and never upgrades it. Other code comes to depend on its exact behaviour, the effect known as Hyrum's Law: "With a sufficient number of users of an API, it does not matter what you promise in the contract: all observable behaviors of your system will be depended on by somebody" (ch. 1). Years later a security disclosure forces the upgrade: "Nobody in this scenario has practice in performing the upgrade, and the upgrade is extra difficult because it is covering many smaller releases covering the entire period between initial introduction of the package ... and the security disclosure" (Software Engineering at Google, ch. 21). The same book names the trap that follows a painful upgrade: teams overestimate the next one, decide "never again", and stop upgrading at all (ch. 1).

Public accounts show what a large catch-up costs. GitHub's move from Rails 3.2 to 5.2 took a year and a half, began as a part-time effort with no dedicated team, and was shaped by end of life: GitHub had been backporting security fixes to 3.2 itself, so it could only deploy intermediate versions that were still supported (GitHub, 2018).

Why do teams let it build? The studies agree on the reasons:

  • They do not prioritize it. In a study of over 4,600 GitHub projects, 81.5% kept outdated dependencies, 69% of surveyed developers were unaware of their vulnerable ones, and developers described updating as "extra effort and added responsibility" (Kula et al., 2018).
  • They fear breakage. Breaking changes, understanding what changed, and migration effort top developers' concerns (Mirhosseini and Parnin, 2017; Pashchenko et al., 2020).
  • Starting dependencies are less current. Across 6,374 Maven systems, 81% of dependencies added to an existing system used the latest release at adoption, against 60% of the dependencies a system started with (Kula et al., 2015).

Several measurements suggest that many updates are safer than developers expect, though each measures something narrower than the whole cost of a migration. In Maven Central, 83.4% of library upgrades complied with semantic versioning and 7.9% of clients were affected by a statically detected breaking change (Ochoa et al., 2022). Across 18 ecosystems, survey respondents reported a median of less than once a year for breaking changes affecting their packages (Bogart et al., 2021). The other end is real: naive updates caused at least one compile or test failure in 28% of sampled Java modules in one experiment, which its authors call a lower bound (Dann et al., 2023).

SynthesisTest confidence is one plausible reason the fear persists: a team that cannot tell whether an upgrade broke something will rationally avoid upgrading. Tests catch only part of what an update can break (see below), so static change-impact analysis, integration tests, monitoring and staged rollout belong beside coverage.

You now know how the debt accrues and why teams let it. Next: what the tools meant to help do to developers' attention.

Update bots raise upgrade rates and can exhaust attention

Automated pull requests work, on average. Projects using them upgraded 1.6 times as often as projects using no tool, and projects using a status badge 1.4 times as often (Mirhosseini and Parnin, 2017). After teams adopted Dependabot, average technical lag fell from about 49 days to 25 within 90 days (He et al., 2023).

Merge rates vary widely by sample, so no single number describes "bot acceptance":

StudySampleBot pull requests merged
Mirhosseini and Parnin, 20172,578 projects using GreenkeeperAbout one third
Alfadel et al., 20212,904 JavaScript projects, security updates65%; test failures explained 3.2% of the unmerged ones
Mohayeji et al., 2023npm projects, security updates57%
He et al., 20231,823 well-engineered projects70% of version updates; 74% to 76% of security updates
Rebatchi et al., 2024Large multi-language sample, security updates25%, against 74% for security pull requests opened by people (figures as extracted by the literature screen)

Notes: the samples differ in ecosystem, project maturity and period. When a security update is not merged, developers usually fix the vulnerability by hand, and that path often takes months (Mohayeji et al., 2023).

The cost lands on attention. The first named failure modes were update suspicion (doubt that a bot's change is safe) and notification fatigue (Mirhosseini and Parnin, 2017). In the largest Dependabot study, teams commonly reconfigured the bot to reduce notifications and 11.3% of projects dropped it, most often for notification fatigue, a lack of grouped updates, or incompatibility with their package manager. Most that left moved to another bot, usually Renovate (He et al., 2023). Much of the noise is waste: 22.6% of Dependabot updates in one Java study targeted dependencies the code did not use (Soto-Valero et al., 2021), and across 1,487 npm projects, updates to unused dependencies triggered 55.9% of all dependency-update build time (Weeraddana et al., 2024).

The remedies with the best support:

  1. Remove unused dependencies first. It shrinks both the attack surface and the update stream.
  2. Group and schedule updates. Dependabot added grouped version updates in August 2023 and grouped security updates in March 2024 (GitHub changelog). Renovate's maintainers describe the usual arc, from "feel the power of automation" to "this is getting overwhelming" within weeks, and recommend grouping, schedules outside working hours and automatic merging, with the goal that "you should 'see' Renovate less and less" (Renovate docs). Grouping has a cost: a failed group takes longer to diagnose.
  3. Merge automatically when tests pass, for every kind of update a person would merge anyway. Developers already behave this way: in one survey, 85% merged bot updates when tests passed and updated by hand otherwise (He et al., 2023).
  4. Know the limits of the tests. In one study, test suites covered under 60% of direct dependency calls in half of projects and caught 47% of injected faults in direct dependencies and 35% in transitive ones; static change-impact analysis caught 74% and 64% (Hejderup and Gousios, 2021).
Evidence gapThis review's searches found no study after 2023 that measures whether grouping reduced bot abandonment or raised merge rates. Release-age cooldowns, which delay adopting brand-new releases to dodge malicious ones, now have an adoption study (Tanaka et al., 2026) and incident-based analyses showing a short delay would have blocked specific malicious releases (Datadog Security Labs). Controlled evidence of their security effect and their cost in slower patching is still limited.

Prevention starts at selection, and it is informal. In interviews and a survey of 115 developers, 44% rated active maintenance a highly influential factor when choosing a library, against 39% for security (Larios Vargas et al., 2020). Revealed choices track something else: the npm packages developers pick correlate with downloads, stars and the length of the README (Mujahid et al., 2022), all of which an abandoned package keeps. Health scores are a weak signal so far. In OpenSSF Scorecard studies, the Maintained check ranks among the practices most associated with vulnerability counts, but the models explain 9% to 12% of the variance, and reported vulnerabilities rose with aggregate score, likely because better-run projects find and report more (Zahan et al., 2023).

You now know what bots and selection screens do for developers and to them. Next: the costs that have nothing to do with security.

Beyond security, end of life costs operability, money and speed

Dependency currency is a general quality attribute, and the delivery research files it that way. The DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA) program places "keep dependencies up-to-date" inside its code maintainability capability, a predictor of delivery performance, and names the common failure: "most organizations leave it up to teams to manage their dependencies, with highly variable results", so that finding the services a new vulnerability affects becomes "a substantial archaeology project" (DORA). DORA's construct mixes dependency items with code search and reuse, so its data does not isolate dependency freshness on its own.

The other costs show up in concrete ways:

  • Operability. Managed platforms enforce end of life by freezing change. AWS Lambda first blocks creating functions on a deprecated runtime and later blocks updating them, except to move to a supported runtime (AWS). AWS allows a grace period after deprecation, but once the update-blocking date passes, a function can still run while an urgent fix to it first requires a runtime migration, which turns a quiet debt into an incident-time blocker.
  • Defects. Outdated packages in Debian-based container images carried more known bugs as well as more vulnerabilities (Zerouali et al., 2019).
  • Performance and cost. Instagram reported 12% fewer CPU instructions per request on its web tier after moving from Python 2 to 3 (PyCon 2017 keynote; practitioner). Amazon reported that moving applications to Java 17 with an AI assistant cut the average upgrade from about 50 developer-days to hours and estimated 4,500 developer-years saved (as reported); that figure is self-reported by the company that sells the tool.
  • Developer time. A vendor-commissioned survey put maintenance, debugging and technical debt at about 17 hours of a developer's week (Stripe, The Developer Coefficient, 2018; secondary). It does not separate dependency work.
Evidence gapThis review's searches found no study that links dependency freshness directly to incident rates, change failure rate or developer satisfaction. The links above are mechanisms and case reports, not measured effects across organizations.

You now know the full cost picture. Next: the approach the published accounts converge on.

Published accounts converge on continuous upgrades paid for by the producer

The organizations that published their experience describe a similar move: from episodic catch-up projects to small, continuous, automated upgrades on shared tooling. They differ on who reviews each change and who does the migration work. These are primary-industry accounts, self-reported and unaudited.

  • Google made infrastructure teams responsible for moving their own users to new versions in 2012, its "Churn Rule": "having a dedicated group of experts execute the change scales better than asking for more maintenance effort from every user" (ch. 1). Its deprecation chapter finds that warnings "rarely lead to migration of existing systems", that compulsory deprecation needs "an enforcement mechanism", and that it works best when a specialized team staffs the migration to completion (ch. 15).
  • Spotify moved to fleet management, in which the platform team that ships a change rolls it out across every repository that uses it. Spotify says it does not want "a single 'refactoring team' responsible for rolling out other teams' changes". A new version of its internal service framework once took about 200 days to reach 70% of backend services and now takes under 7; automation had merged over 300,000 changes, about 75% without a person in the loop; and a Log4j fix reached 80% of production backend services within 9 hours (Spotify, part 1; part 3). Automatic merging is opt-out, and component owners receive changes they were not consulted on first.
  • GitHub went from an 18-month Rails catch-up to an automated pull request every Monday that bumps the application to the latest commit on the Rails main branch; "nearly every week" one of its deploys is a Rails upgrade (GitHub, 2023).
  • Shopify runs its main application on the unreleased edge of Rails with weekly automated bumps, so it does not "dedicate a whole team and months of work" to each upgrade, behind more than 130,000 tests and canary deploys. Every Monday its engineers "go over the new commits merged upstream" before shipping (Shopify, 2019).

The common elements across the four are frequent small updates, automation that opens the change, a large test suite, deployment safeguards such as canaries, and a small set of standard frameworks to keep current. Human review varies: Spotify merges most fleet changes automatically, while Shopify reviews each week's upstream commits by hand. AI-assisted migration lowers the cost of the hard cases. Across 39 migrations at Google over twelve months, a language model generated 74% of the submitted code changes, and developers estimated the total time spent fell by half; the authors call it a case study, not a controlled comparison (Ziftci et al., 2025). Research tools that repair breaking updates report high success on benchmarks, but none has production-scale adoption evidence yet.

Evidence gapThis review's searches found no empirical study that compares centralized approaches, such as platform-owned upgrades or monorepos with one version per library, against team-by-team ownership. Curated package sets have been studied on their own terms, for example the Stackage set for Haskell (Leger et al., 2023), but not as an organizational intervention. Everything above is organizations reporting on themselves.

You now know what the published accounts describe. Next: how to choose a response for a dependency that is already at end of life.

Five responses, chosen by what is still possible

SynthesisThis section combines the evidence above into a decision aid. It is reasoning from the sources, not a tested method.

When a dependency reaches end of life or stops being maintained, five responses are available. They are listed in the order this brief suggests considering them.

ResponseWhen it fitsWhat it costsEvidence
Upgrade or migrate to the supported lineA supported successor existsThe accumulated debt; least when upgrades have been continuousPrimary-industry accounts above
Replace with a maintained alternativeThe project is abandoned and a credible alternative existsMigration effort and new selection riskThe most common response developers report (Miller et al., 2023)
Fork, or copy the code in, and own itNo alternative exists and the code is small or stableOngoing maintenance for as long as the code is usedCopied files seldom record their origin; among observable vulnerable copies, the median gap from upstream fix to downstream change was 640 days (He et al., 2026)
Buy extended supportMigration cannot finish before support endsLicence cost; a dependency on the support vendorVendor offerings only; no independent outcome data found
Isolate and accept, with an expiry dateNothing else is feasible yetResidual risk, carried visiblyNIST SA-22 discusses isolation for kept components; PCI DSS 12.3.4 requires an approved remediation plan; the expiry date is this brief's recommendation

Choose by asking, in order: is there a supported successor; is there a maintained alternative; how much of the library the code calls; how long until a migration could finish; and who will own the result. Three rules make any choice hold:

  1. Every exception expires. A risk acceptance without a date becomes permanent by default.
  2. Someone owns the dependency. Google's engineering book illustrates the problem with a scenario in which a package's ownership file still lists two engineers, one of whom has moved to management and one to another team (ch. 21). An ownership record needs a periodic check that the named people still work on the code.
  3. The platform owns the common path. Runtimes, base images and the standard frameworks belong to a team that keeps them current for everyone, the way a paved road is maintained for its users.

What to measure. DORA's suggested measures transfer directly: the share of applications not on the latest stable version of everything they use, how many versions of each library run in production and how many are over a year old, and how often and how long upgrades take (DORA). Add two that capture end of life itself: the number of end-of-life components in production, from lifecycle data rather than vulnerability data, and the count of open exceptions past their expiry date.

Five misreadings keep teams waiting

  • "No known vulnerabilities, so it is safe." An end-of-life component is clean only until the next disclosure, and then nobody upstream will fix it. The risk is forward-looking.
  • "Our scanner would tell us." Vulnerability matching does not see support lifecycles. Some tools add lifecycle checks, often only for operating systems, so check what yours covers.
  • "Upgrades break things, so stay put." Most upgrades studied follow semantic versioning, and few clients are hit by the breaking changes that do occur. Staying put tends to make the eventual upgrade larger.
  • "A deprecation notice will move people." Not reliably. Some widely used deprecated packages keep gaining users, and Google found warnings rarely produce migration. Deadlines, enforcement and a staffed migration work better.
  • "Turning on an update bot solves it." Bots raise upgrade rates, but without grouping, pruning and trustworthy tests they generate noise that teams learn to ignore or switch off.

Check that you can explain it

What is the difference between technical lag and terminal lag?

Technical lag is the gap between the versions in use and the newest ones, which closes when the team upgrades. Terminal lag is the same gap when the upstream is abandoned: no new version will come, so waiting never closes it.

Why can a reachability analysis lower the urgency of a finding but not the risk of an end-of-life library?

Reachability says whether the application calls the vulnerable code in today's known flaw. An end-of-life library's risk includes the next flaw, which may sit in code the application does call, and which nobody upstream will fix. Reachability and isolation still help decide which library to deal with first.

A team has pinned an old major version of a well-maintained library. Is it exposed to end-of-life risk?

Possibly. In one large study most vulnerabilities affecting several major lines were fixed only in the newest one, so an old major line may get no security fixes even though the project is alive. Check that line's support policy and patch history.

What do the published accounts of continuous upgrading have in common?

Frequent small updates, automation that opens the change, a large test suite, and deployment safeguards such as canaries. At Google and Spotify, the team that ships a change also migrates its users. They differ on human review: Spotify merges most changes automatically, while Shopify reviews upstream commits by hand each week.

Glossary

End of life
The point after which a maintainer or vendor has announced it will no longer fix bugs or vulnerabilities in a release line.
Unmaintained
A project whose maintainers have stopped work without announcing it; also called abandoned.
Technical lag
How far a project's dependencies trail their latest releases, in versions or in time.
Terminal lag
Technical lag that can never close, because the upstream is abandoned.
Libyear
A lag measure: the summed age of each dependency in use compared with its newest release.
Semantic versioning
A version-numbering convention in which only a major version change may break compatibility.
Software composition analysis
Tools that inventory a project's third-party components and match them against vulnerability databases; some also check licences and support lifecycles.
Fleet management
Spotify's term for platform teams authoring and rolling out changes across every repository at once.
Hyrum's Law
With enough users, every observable behaviour of a system will be depended on by someone.

How this was made

Question. How are end-of-life and unmaintained libraries handled, seen from risk, security, technical-debt management and developer experience together? Set by Julie Davila on 29 October 2026, as a companion to the paving research.

Process. An orchestrating agent split the question into three lines (security and risk, technical-debt economics, developer experience) and gave each to a research agent working from the public web, with a budget of 30 searches and 25 page reads per line. In parallel, two structured literature screens in Elicit each included 40 peer-reviewed or preprint studies (27 full texts read in each). The security line's agent stalled, so its standards and incident material was gathered directly by the orchestrating agent from search results and primary pages. The orchestrating agent reconciled the notes and drafted this brief; Julie Davila reviewed it before publication.

What was checked and what was not. Quotations from Google's engineering book, GitHub, Shopify, Renovate, DORA and the arXiv papers were read in the source. Figures for studies marked "abstract only", the vendor reports, and several standards summaries come from abstracts, search results or secondary pages, as marked. The research-process account above is this site's own record and cannot be checked against public sources.

Adversarial review and corrections (version 1.1, 29 October 2026). A model from a different vendor (OpenAI GPT, run read-only through Codex) checked every load-bearing claim against its sources. It confirmed more than 40 claims and raised 27 findings. The most consequential were checked again against the primary texts before any change. Version 1.1 corrects: the Black Duck figure (now the 2026 report's 93%, without a cross-year trend); the Greenkeeper sample (2,578 projects); the 3.2% figure (test failures among unmerged pull requests, not a breakage rate); the NIST and PCI requirements (neither prescribes expiring risk acceptance; that is this brief's recommendation); the Cyber Resilience Act support period (at least five years, tied to expected use, from December 2027); halted libraries (a fix may exist underneath); the Spotify and Shopify accounts (producer-owned migrations, and human review at Shopify); and several overclaims about breakage, test confidence, scorecards, deprecation and evidence gaps. A 42.7% npm figure was removed because its primary paper could not be found. Gap statements now say they reflect this review's searches.

Model and tools. Anthropic Claude agents for planning, search, reading and drafting; Elicit for the literature screens; the html-house-style kit for this page.
